The gap

The core mechanismAssumption is sticky. It only moves when the team gets real information back.

What breaks down for a team is a gap between assumption and fact.

People are consistently overconfident about how well they understand each other, and that confidence doesn't correct on its own. Research on perspective-taking finds that accuracy improves when people receive real information about each other, not when they simply try harder to imagine another point of view. Guessing alone doesn't close the gap. Finding out does — which is why a Moment ends with the whole team learning the answer at the same time, rather than one person collecting insight about everyone else.

Why the usual fixes miss

Two dead endsProximity without structure, and measurement without action.

Putting people in a room doesn't do it. Neither does another survey.

The proximity paradox

Bernstein and Turban tracked Fortune 500 teams moving from cubicles to open-plan offices using sociometric badges. Face-to-face interaction fell by roughly 70% — from about 5.8 hours a day to 1.7 — while electronic messaging rose 20% to 50%. Exposed to constant observation, people build a fourth wall: headphones on, eyes down. Proximity without structured intent produces withdrawal, not connection.

The insights-to-action gap

Engagement platforms are very good at identifying dysfunction and offer nothing to remediate it. Qualtrics found that while 92% of employees think it matters to be heard, only 7% believe their organization acts on feedback efficiently. What gets called survey fatigue is really inaction fatigue — and asking without acting actively erodes trust rather than holding it steady.

Why play

Not a nice-to-havePlay is the mechanism, not the sweetener. It's what makes the practice safe enough to work.

Play is what makes the rest of it possible.

A game creates what Johan Huizinga called the magic circle: a bounded space where the ordinary rules — seniority, reporting lines, who usually speaks first — are suspended by agreement. Inside it, a junior analyst can contradict a director without it being insubordination, because it's a round, not a meeting. That suspension is what the rest of the work rests on.

Safety comes before candor

Amy Edmondson's work established psychological safety — the shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk — as the condition that lets learning behavior happen at all. Play manufactures that condition quickly and cheaply, in a setting where being wrong costs nothing.

Disclosure has to escalate

Aron's closeness-generation research found that structured self-disclosure builds real closeness when it escalates gradually. The escalation is the method, not a detail of it — which is why a Season is sequenced across ten weeks rather than delivered in one long session.

Rhythm beats the offsite

Learning consolidates through spaced repetition. An annual offsite is massed practice: intense, then gone. Fifteen minutes a week is the schedule that actually overwrites a team's default behavior.

What team-building can honestly claim

Klein and colleagues' meta-analysis across 60 correlations found a moderate positive effect for team-building overall, strongest on affective and process outcomes. We design for what the evidence supports, and measure the rest rather than assert it.
